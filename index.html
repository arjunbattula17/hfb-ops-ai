What this system does

Each morning, this tool turns Harris-County food-insecurity data, Houston Food Bank's real partner-agency network, USDA/FDA cold-chain rules, and real Harris County drive times into a concrete, checkable plan: how much food goes where, on which truck, in what order — before a human signs off and trucks roll.

It is a decision-support tool, not an autonomous dispatcher. Every plan below requires explicit human approval on this page before it is marked ready for dispatch.

Risk assessment

Ethical review

No demographic inputs

The allocation formula never takes race, ethnicity, or immigration status as an input. It weights strictly by food-insecurity rate, itself modeled from publicly reported poverty data. Feeding America's own Harris County research shows food insecurity is highly correlated with race (Black 34%, Latino 25%, non-Hispanic White 11%) [Map the Meal Gap 2025] — so a poverty-weighted, need-based formula structurally routes more aid to the communities that research shows are most affected, without ever encoding identity directly.

Transparency over black-box optimization

Every number on this site traces to a visible source tag (sourced / computed / modeled). There is no hidden ML model — the allocation is an auditable formula an operations director can check by hand in 10 minutes. See Data & Methodology.

Human-in-the-loop, not automated dispatch

The system stops at "recommended plan." A person must review the Risk tab and click Approve before anything is marked ready. This is enforced in the UI, not just written policy.

Unmet need is reported, not hidden

When agency or fleet capacity can't absorb all measured need, the gap is surfaced as a KPI and a risk item — not silently dropped from the totals. Hiding shortfalls would make the system look more successful than it is, at the cost of the communities actually going without.

Dignity in distribution design

Allocation is computed at the agency level, not the individual level — no client-level data is collected, scored, or stored by this system. Partner agencies retain full discretion over how food is distributed to individuals (choice-model pantry, drive-through, etc.).

Known limitation: proxy bias

Poverty rate is a proxy for food insecurity, not a direct measurement — Map the Meal Gap itself only publishes county-level rates, so ZIP-level figures here are modeled (see Methodology). A proxy can be wrong for a specific neighborhood. This is why every "modeled" data point is labeled as such, and why the human approval gate exists.

Suggested improvements

  1. ZIP-level food-insecurity data. Commission or partner on a local study — Map the Meal Gap tops out at county resolution, so every ZIP-level rate here is modeled, not measured.
  2. More reefer capacity, urgently. Under the FDA's stricter 60-minute rule that actually applies on hot Houston afternoons, today's 2-truck reefer fleet can safely reach only 3 of 13 zones directly — the single highest-severity item in Risk Assessment. Insulated (non-mechanical) cold-box kits for near-in-radius stops and/or 1-2 additional reefer trucks would close most of this gap.
  3. Live traffic integration. Today's drive times are free-flow OSRM estimates with a disclosed 1.25x congestion assumption — a live traffic API would replace an assumption with a measurement.
  4. Partner-agency capacity grants for capped zones. Every zone flagged "capacity-constrained" in Fairness Analysis has real need beyond what its partner agency can currently store or process — that's an infrastructure gap, not an allocation problem.
  5. A backup depot. Today's plan has a single point of failure at the Portwall Street warehouse (see Risk Assessment). Not solvable in a one-day plan, but worth tracking.
  6. Feedback loop from agencies. Actual pounds received/used/wasted per agency would let the model self-correct instead of re-deriving need from poverty proxies every day.

Plain-language explainer, for staff

What did the system decide today? It looked at 13 neighborhoods we serve, figured out roughly how many people in each one are likely food insecure, and split today's trucks and food so that neighborhoods with more need get more food — but never more than what that neighborhood's pantry can actually store and hand out in a day.

Why does one neighborhood get more than another? Two reasons, and they're both visible on this site: (1) it has a higher estimated share of food-insecure residents, or (2) a nearby zone hit its pantry's storage limit, so its extra share got moved to a zone with room.

What's "cold chain" and why do I keep seeing it? Perishable food (meat, dairy, produce) has to stay cold or it becomes unsafe to eat — federal rules say it can only sit in the 41–135°F "danger zone" for 2 hours max, or just 1 hour once it's hotter than 90°F outside (which is most Houston August afternoons) before it should be tossed. We plan routes to stay well under that, so a truck stuck in traffic doesn't turn into wasted, unsafe food.

What does "unmet need" mean, and should I be alarmed? It means today's trucks and our partner pantries' storage together can't absorb everything the data says is needed. It's not hidden — it's a real gap, and it's the strongest argument for the capacity-building items in Suggested Improvements.

Do I have to trust the computer? No. Every number links back to where it came from (see Methodology), and nothing gets dispatched until a person clicks Approve on the Overview page.

Data & methodology

Allocation formula

  1. Estimate each zone's food-insecure population: population × modeled local food-insecurity rate
  2. Local rate = county rate (18.2%) × (zone poverty rate ÷ county poverty baseline 19.6%), clamped to 3–45%
  3. Ideal allocation = zone's share of total network need × total fleet capacity (64,000 lbs)
  4. Cap at the zone's partner-agency intake capacity; redistribute capped overflow to zones with headroom (3 rounds)
  5. Split each zone's allocation 28% cold / 72% dry (typical food-bank product mix, disclosed assumption)

Routing & cold-chain rule

Route time = (farthest stop's OSRM drive time × 1.25 congestion factor) + 12 min × number of stops. Above 90°F ambient — the Houston August norm — FDA's danger-zone threshold tightens from 2 hours to 60 minutes. Refrigerated routes here are capped at 45 minutes, a real safety margin under the threshold that actually governs a day like this one, not the more lenient 120-minute rule.
